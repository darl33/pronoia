Pre-positioning in Australian utility networks: an update on Volt Typhoon

Published 2026-04-22

Summary

Between November 2025 and March 2026 we responded to intrusions at four
organisations across the Australian water and energy sectors, including two
regional water treatment operators and a distribution network service provider.
We attribute this activity to Volt Typhoon. The intrusions share tooling,
tradecraft and infrastructure with previously reported campaigns by the same
group, and in each case the operators appeared to be establishing durable access
rather than pursuing immediate disruption or theft.

Initial access

In three of the four intrusions the operators authenticated to internet-facing
remote administration portals using credentials that were valid, unexpired and
belonged to real staff accounts. There was no exploitation and no malware on the
perimeter device. The accounts had been enrolled in the corporate directory for
years and, in two cases, belonged to contractors who had not worked with the
organisation since 2023. In the fourth intrusion the operators connected through
a legacy VPN concentrator that had been left reachable from the internet after a
network refresh, using a service account that was never intended for interactive
logon.

Once inside, the operators worked almost entirely with software already present
on the hosts. We recovered no dropper, no implant and no compiled payload from
any of the affected environments.

Discovery and lateral movement

The first commands executed on each beachhead host enumerated the local system:
installed patches, network adapter configuration, mapped drives, running
services and the local administrator group. On two hosts these commands were
issued through the built-in scripting shell, invoked with an encoded command
line so that the arguments did not appear in plain text in the process
telemetry. The operators then moved between hosts using the native remote
desktop client, reusing the same valid credentials rather than harvesting new
ones.

Command and control

Outbound traffic from the affected environments was relayed through compromised
small-office routers on residential Australian and New Zealand ISP ranges before
reaching operator infrastructure, a pattern consistent with T1090 and one that
substantially complicates network-based detection: to a defender reviewing
netflow, the traffic resembles ordinary domestic broadband. We observed relay
traffic to 192[.]0[.]2[.]117 and to 198[.]51[.]100[.]42, and a single beacon to
hxxps://cdn-status[.]water-ops[.]example over 443.

Assessment

We assess with high confidence that the objective was persistent access to
operational technology support networks rather than data theft. No data
staging, archiving or exfiltration was observed in any of the four intrusions,
and the operators repeatedly took steps to remain within the noise floor of
normal administrative activity. Organisations in the water and energy sectors
should audit remote access accounts belonging to former contractors and review
authentication logs for service accounts used interactively.
